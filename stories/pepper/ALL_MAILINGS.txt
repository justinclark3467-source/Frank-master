﻿Dearest Ma,
I am writing this sitting on a wooden crate with my helmet turned upside down beside me to catch what little light is left. It is not much of a desk, but it does the job well enough. The sun is dropping behind the hills now, and for a moment the way the light hits the water reminds me of the East River. I found myself staring at it longer than I should have, almost expecting to see the city on the other side instead of this place. It is strange how the mind works when it wants to be somewhere else.
The trip over felt longer than it probably was. Days ran together in a way that made it hard to keep track of time, and nights did not feel much different. Sleep came when it could, and when it did not, you just lay there listening to the ship creak and the water move against the hull. Some of the fellows tried to joke their way through it, while others kept to themselves. I spent most of that time thinking about home, about the kitchen, the way the light comes through the window in the morning, and the smell when you start cooking before anyone else is awake.
When we finally made land, there was no great sense of arrival, only orders, movement, and men trying to keep up with whatever was happening around them. The ground felt different right away, heavier somehow, and the air carried a smell that is hard to describe, something like salt and earth mixed together, with oil and smoke always underneath it. You get used to it quicker than you would expect, though you never stop noticing it.
Italy is beautiful, Ma, but it’s broken. The hills roll on farther than you would think, and there are old stone houses set into them that look like they have been there longer than anything we have known. Some still stand untouched, while others are broken open, walls gone and roofs caved in. We moved through a village not long after we landed that looked like people had just stepped out and never came back. Laundry still hanging on lines, doors left open, chairs tipped over in the street. No one there. That kind of quiet stays with you.
I have been staying close to a couple of the boys from back home. Sal and Petey are both here, and it helps more than I can explain just to hear familiar voices now and then. There is also Russo, who talks the same way he always has, like he is still on a street corner in Brooklyn instead of a hillside here. He has already tried to start a card game more than once, even with shells landing close enough to shake the ground. It sounds foolish when you say it out loud, but it keeps the air from getting too heavy.
We have been moving in short stretches, holding where we are told, then moving again. Nothing feels settled. The Germans have the higher ground in a lot of places, and they make you work for every step. You learn quickly to keep your head down and your eyes open. There is always something you do not see if you get careless.
There was a moment today that caught me off guard. We were moving along the edge of a broken stone wall, the kind that looks like it has been there for generations, and right in the middle of it there was a patch of wild herbs growing through the cracks. I could not tell you what they were, but the smell of it stopped me where I stood. For a second I was not here anymore. I was back in the kitchen, watching you cook, waiting for you to turn your back so I could steal something off the counter. Then one of the fellows called out and I snapped out of it.
Not far from that wall, I noticed something else. One of the stones had a mark carved into it, not from damage, but something deliberate. A small circle with a short line running through it, clean edges, not worn like the rest. I saw the same mark again farther down the grove, cut into another stone at about the same height. It could be nothing, just something left behind long before we ever got here, but it stayed with me.
Nights are different here. During the day there is always something happening, something to focus on. At night, everything slows down. The men get quiet. You sit and wait, and every man listens for something he hopes he does not hear. I keep your rosary tucked in my pocket, right over my heart. I have not taken it off since I left.
There are things here I do not understand yet. Not just the fighting, but the way parts of this land feel untouched while everything around them is not. I will not try to guess at it yet. I would rather understand it properly before I start telling stories about it.
Do not worry about me more than you have to. I am being careful, and I am staying close to the men I trust. When things are quiet, I think about home.
You just keep taking care of yourself. Write when you can and tell me the small things. Those are the ones that stay with me the longest.
Your loving son,
 Frank

Dearest Ma,
The rain has settled in here in a way that makes it feel like it has no intention of ever leaving. It is not the kind that comes down hard and clears the air, but something slower and heavier that works its way into everything. The ground turns to a thick red mud that clings to your boots until each step feels like you are carrying more than your own weight. After a while you feel it in your legs and your back, and even the wagons seem to groan under it.
We have been holding near a grove just outside what used to be a village. Lemon trees mostly, or what is left of them. Many of the branches are broken now, hanging low and split, but when the wind shifts the smell still comes through. It cuts through everything else, through the damp and the smoke, and for a moment it almost feels like something normal. It reminded me of your kitchen again this morning, the way the place smells when you have just started cooking and the whole room feels warmer than the day outside.
There has been more movement these past few days, though not the kind a man would speak about with any pride. Orders come down, we move a little, then wait, then move again. The Germans are still holding the better ground around here, and they make sure we feel it every time we try to push forward. You begin to understand quickly that even a small piece of land can cost more than it looks like it should.
I found myself back near that grove again when things slowed down. I am not sure why I keep going there, except that some places stay with you in a way others do not. There is something about it that feels different from the rest of the ground around it. Not safer, exactly, but not the same either.
I went back into the cellar of the villa I told you about. The place is mostly broken now, but the cellar is still intact enough to keep things dry. I spent some time looking through that book again. The more I study it, the less it feels like something ordinary. There are drawings of plants and roots, things that look like they belong in a garden, but there are markings in the margins that do not fit with the rest of it. Small notations, repeated in ways that feel deliberate rather than careless.
Some pages are scratched over, not worn but cut into, like someone tried to remove something without tearing the page out completely. One page is missing altogether, taken clean from the binding. It does not feel like damage. It feels like a decision.
On my way back through the grove, I saw that mark again. The small circle with the line through it, carved low into a stone where the ground dips slightly. The first time I noticed it, I thought it might have been chance. The second time, I knew it was not.
And I saw someone as well.
Not clearly enough to say much more than that, but enough to know I had not imagined it before. A figure moving between the trees, not in a hurry, but certain. That is the part that stayed with me. Not fear, not confusion. Certainty. Whoever it was knew exactly where they were stepping.
I did not call out, and I did not follow. There are moments when pushing forward too quickly gets you less than waiting, and I have learned enough here to know the difference.
The fellows are holding up as well as can be expected. Russo still talks too much, Sal still pretends not to worry while worrying anyway, and Petey still keeps both of them from making fools of themselves. We find small things to laugh at when we can. It matters more than I would have believed before coming here.
At night, things quiet down just enough to make every small sound feel louder. A man shifting his weight, a boot pulling free from the mud, the wind catching broken branches. You sit and listen and try not to let your thoughts run too far ahead. I keep your rosary where I always do, and I reach for it more often than I would ever say aloud.
There are things here that do not fit together the way they should. The marks in the stone, the book in the cellar, the feeling that parts of this place are being used for something beyond what the rest of us can see. I am not going to turn myself into a storyteller over it, but I am not going to ignore it either.
Do not worry about me. I am being careful. I am watching where I step, and I am staying close to the men I trust.
You just keep taking care of yourself. Write when you can and tell me about home, about the little things most of all. Those are what I carry around with me.
Your loving son,
 Frank

The ground looked no different from anywhere else, but she stepped with certainty, placing each foot as if she already knew where it would land. You just keep taking care of yourself. That is what I carry with me more than anything else.
Your loving son,
