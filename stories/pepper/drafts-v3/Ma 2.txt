Dearest Ma,

The rain has settled in here in a way that makes it feel like it has no intention of ever leaving. It is not the kind that comes down hard and clears the air, but something slower and heavier that works its way into everything. The ground turns to a thick red mud that clings to your boots until each step feels like you are carrying more than your own weight. After a while you feel it in your legs and your back, and even the wagons seem to groan under it.

We have been holding near a grove just outside what used to be a village. Lemon trees mostly, or what is left of them. Many of the branches are broken now, hanging low and split, but when the wind shifts the smell still comes through. It cuts through everything else, through the damp and the smoke, and for a moment it almost feels like something normal. It reminded me of your kitchen again this morning, the way the place smells when you have just started cooking and the whole room feels warmer than the day outside.

There has been more movement these past few days, though not the kind a man would speak about with any pride. Orders come down, we move a little, then wait, then move again. The Germans are still holding the better ground around here, and they make sure we feel it every time we try to push forward. You begin to understand quickly that even a small piece of land can cost more than it looks like it should.

I found myself back near that grove again when things slowed down. I am not sure why I keep going there, except that some places stay with you in a way others do not. There is something about it that feels different from the rest of the ground around it. Not safer, exactly, but not the same either.

On my way through, I saw someone.

Just a figure, moving between the trees, not in a hurry, but certain. I could not make out a face at that distance, could not say man or woman, old or young. But there was something in the way they moved — not the nervous hurry of someone fleeing, not the careless ease of someone at home, but something in between. Like someone who knew every step they were taking.

I stopped and watched for a long moment. The figure moved through a section of the grove where I would have expected the ground to give way — soft, unstable, the kind of ground that catches your foot and pulls — but there was no hesitation. No testing. Just steady, certain movement.

Then they were gone. Between one tree and the next, lost in the shadows and the remaining lemon branches, and I could not tell you which direction they went.

I did not call out. There are times when silence serves you better than speech, and this was one of those times.

Not far from where I had stopped, I noticed the mark again. The small circle with the line through it, carved low into a stone where the ground dips slightly. The same mark I saw the first day, carved in the same deliberate way. Someone made that mark, and someone made it to be found.

The fellows are holding up as well as can be expected. Russo still talks too much, Sal still pretends not to worry while worrying anyway, and Petey still keeps both of them from making fools of themselves. We find small things to laugh at when we can. It matters more than I would have believed before coming here.

At night, things quiet down just enough to make every small sound feel louder. A man shifting his weight, a boot pulling free from the mud, the wind catching broken branches. You sit and listen and try not to let your thoughts run too far ahead. I keep your rosary where I always do, and I reach for it more often than I would ever say aloud.

There are things here that do not fit together the way they should. The marks in the stone, the way that figure moved through ground that should not have held. I am not going to turn myself into a storyteller over it, but I am not going to ignore it either.

Do not worry about me. I am being careful. I am watching where I step, and I am staying close to the men I trust.

Write when you can. Tell me about home, about the store, about Joey. The small things are what carry me through.

I have been thinking about the mark in the stone. The circle with the line through it. It showed up again today, in a different spot, but carved the same way. Deliberate. Clean. Someone is leaving these for someone to find. I am starting to believe that someone might be her.

I go back to the villa when I can. The book is becoming clearer, but also stranger. The more I study it, the more I realize it is not about the war at all. It is about something that has been here long before the soldiers came, and will be here long after they are gone.

Your loving son,
Frank
