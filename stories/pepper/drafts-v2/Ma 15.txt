Dearest Ma,

I need to tell you something that I have been putting off. The distraction that started as curiosity has become something else entirely. It has become all I can think about, and it is costing us.

Two nights ago we were moving through a position that should have been simple. A short stretch between two ridges, nothing that should have required more than attention. But my mind was elsewhere — on the grove, on the marks, on whether she was somewhere in those tunnels or somewhere farther. I missed the signal. Just for a second, but that was enough. The Germans pushed a patrol through a gap that would not have been there if I had been paying attention.

No one died because of it, but Sal took a graze to the shoulder. Not bad, but enough to put him out of action for a while. The look he gave me when they pulled him back was not anger. It was worse. It was worry.

Russo finally lost his humor about it. He did not yell, which is how you know it is serious. He just looked at me and said, "You gotta come back to us, Pepper. You're not here alone."

Petey did not say anything. He never does when it matters. But he started staying closer, always within arm's reach when we moved, like he was ready to pull me out of something.

I have been thinking about what they said. About coming back. About the fact that I am not here for the war or for the glory. I am here because I was pulled into it, and the only thing that matters now is getting through it so I can come home.

I have not been back to the grove since that mistake. I cannot afford to be distracted again. The price is too high.

But I think about her. I think about Lucia. I think about whether she is still out there, still moving through those tunnels, still waiting for something or someone. And I wonder if the reason I cannot let her go is because I finally found something in this war that does not feel like war.

The book is with me still. Every night I open it and study the marks. They are not random — they line up with the ground, with the safe places, with the paths that hold. I am beginning to think the book maps more than movement. It maps a world underneath this one, a world that has been here longer than the war, longer than the roads.

The book started as a curiosity, something to pass the time. Now it is an obsession. I think about it during the day, I study it at night. The marks in the margins — the ones that look like herbs and recipes and kitchen notes — they are not what they seem. They are a code. A cipher that maps the tunnels, the safe paths, the places where the earth will hold and the places that will swallow you whole. Someone spent a lifetime recording this knowledge. Someone made sure it would not be lost.

I have not figured out all of it yet. But I am getting closer. And the closer I get, the more I understand why Lucia moved through that grove the way she did. She was not just walking. She was reading a map that has been in her family for generations.

But using the book means thinking about her. And thinking about her means distraction. And distraction means men get hurt.

I am going to try to focus. I am going to try to be the man they need me to be. And when this is all over, I will find my way back to the grove. I will find her.

Until then, I hold the rosary and I think about home. About the store, the kitchen, you making breakfast before the sun is fully up. I think about Joey and the way he pretends to be annoyed when I mess with the register. I think about the smell of your cooking on Sunday mornings and the sound of the bell above the door when a customer walks in.

We are moving again tomorrow. I need to keep my head in the game. I need to be here, fully here, for the men who are counting on me. I cannot let them down. I cannot let you down.

Do not worry about me more than you have to. I am trying to be better.

But I would be lying if I said I was not scared, Ma. I am scared of a lot of things right now. I am scared of not finding her. I am scared of finding her and not being able to let go. I am scared of what the book is trying to show me — because it is showing me something, something underneath this land that goes deeper than I ever imagined.

You just keep taking care of yourself. That is what I carry with me more than anything else. That is what makes me want to come home.

Your loving son,
Frank
