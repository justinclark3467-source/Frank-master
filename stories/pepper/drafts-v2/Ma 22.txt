Dearest Ma,

I am writing this in the kitchen. The kitchen. I am sitting at the table where I have sat my whole life, and I am looking at the window where the morning light comes through, and I am smelling your cooking, and I cannot believe I am here.

We made it. Lucia and I. The ship, the train, the bus, all of it. It took weeks longer than it should have because of my leg, but we made it. And now I am home. I am finally home.

She is here, Ma. Lucia is here. She is standing in the doorway and she is looking at the apartment like it is something from another world, and in a way it is. She has never seen anything like this. She has never been anywhere like this. Everything is so different from the hills of Italy, from the tunnels, from the war. And yet she is here, and she is smiling, and that is all that matters.

I watched her face when she walked in. The way her eyes moved over everything, the way she reached out and touched the counter, the way she looked at the window and then at me and smiled. She said, "It is small, but it is yours."

I told her it was ours.

You should have seen Joey. When we walked through that door, he just stood there. He did not move, did not speak, just stared. And then he crossed the room and grabbed me so hard I thought he would break something, and he held on and he said nothing. Nothing. That is how you know it is real. That is how you know it matters.

And then he looked at Lucia, and he smiled, and he said, "So you are the one."

She laughed. That laugh I have not been able to forget since I heard it in the grove. And she said, "And you must be Joey."

They got along immediately. Of course they did. Joey has that way about him. He knows how to make people feel at home. He knows how to make them laugh. And Lucia — Lucia has a way of making people feel comfortable, even in the strangest situations. It is one of the things I love about her.

I have not told her everything yet. There is time for that. For now, she is learning the store, learning the neighborhood, learning what it is like to live in Brooklyn instead of the hills of Italy. She asks questions about everything, and I love answering them. She wants to know everything — the customers, the routine, the way the register works. She wants to be part of this. She wants to belong.

Tomorrow we are going to show her the city. Coney Island, if my leg can handle it. She has never seen the ocean. I cannot wait to see her face when she sees the waves. I cannot wait to see her feet in the sand. I cannot wait to watch her laugh as the water touches her for the first time.

I am happy, Ma. Truly happy. And I am here.

The book is with me too. The ledger. I brought it home. I do not know what to do with it yet. It feels wrong to keep it hidden, but it also feels wrong to share it. Maybe one day I will tell you the full story. Maybe one day I will show you what it means. But for now, it is in the drawer by my bed, waiting.

I am home. That is all that matters.

The days here are different now. Slower. Peaceful. I wake up and I hear the sounds of the store, the rumble of the neighborhood, the voices of neighbors I have not seen in years. And I think about what we went through. The tunnels. The war. The families we saved. The people we lost. All of it led to this moment. All of it brought me here, to this kitchen, to this life.

And I would do it all again.

Lucia is adjusting. She is learning the rhythms of the store, the neighborhood, the city. She is a quick learner, and she has a good eye for the work. She helps Joey with the register, and she talks to the customers in her broken English, and they love her. Everyone loves her.

I watch her sometimes, Ma. I watch her laugh with you at the table, or help Joey with the books, or stand at the window looking out at the street. And I think about the grove. About the tunnels. About the war. About how close I came to losing her. And I think about how lucky I am.

I am the luckiest man in the world.

Thank you for waiting for me, Ma. Thank you for keeping the light on.

Your loving son,
Frank
