Dearest Grandmother,

I am writing to you again tonight, confused and frightened as never before. There are machinations closing around me, and I cannot shake the feeling that I am being watched, noted, judged.

It started at the Whitmore dinner. Senator Whitmore hosted a grand affair, half of Chicago society there in silks and jewels. I was seated beside the young Whitmore, his conversation entirely about horses, while I watched Vivian Penworth several places down. Her eyes drifted in my direction, that slight curl of her lips, that satisfied smile that never reaches her eyes. She was speaking of me. I knew it like a stone in my stomach.

After dinner, I cornered Constance Belmont in the ladies' retiring room. She went pale when I asked about any interesting talk. In a whisper so quiet I had to lean close, she told me Vivian had been saying I had been seen in peculiar parts of the city, visiting strange establishments, spending time with a man of questionable reputation and no visible means of support. Vivian described me as "changed," "absent from so many functions," and wondered aloud whether there was anything wrong, whether there were "reasons" my family ought to know about.

When I arrived home, Father was waiting in the study. He asked where I had been, and I said Constance, as always. He asked if I had enjoyed the dinner, if I had spoken to the young Whitmore. Then he leaned forward with that grave expression I know too well: "I expect an announcement soon, Eleanor. The Whitmore family has made their interest known. It would be wise to encourage the match. A young man like that, with his family's connections — there are not many opportunities like this."

I wanted to scream that I did not care about their connections or their money, that I had found someone who made me feel alive. But I could not say these things. Not to Father. So I nodded, said I was grateful for his guidance, and left with my heart pounding and dread settling over me like a dark cloud.

I went to see Basil the next day. But when I arrived, the door hung ajar, the staircase dark, and his workshop was chaos — papers thrown everywhere, drawings torn from the walls, the cot overturned. He sat at his workbench with his head in his hands.

Someone had broken in the night. Torn through his things looking for something, and they had found it. His blueprints for the telephone — months of work, detailed schematics — gone. Stolen. Along with his notebooks, his calculations. Anyone could claim them now.

I asked who would do such a thing, and he shook his head. "Perhaps rivals. Perhaps industrialists who don't want to see my ideas succeed. It doesn't matter who. The result is the same: my work is gone, and I have no way to prove what was mine."

I wanted to tell him we would rebuild what had been lost. But I knew it was not true. The world is not kind to men like Basil Crain. And I am beginning to wonder whether it is kind to women like me either.

I asked him who could have done such a thing, who would want to steal his work, and he shook his head and said he did not know — perhaps rivals, perhaps industrialists who did not want to see his ideas succeed, perhaps men who worked for the very companies that Father respects and admires. He said it didn't matter who had done it, because the result was the same: his work was gone, and he had no way to prove what he had invented, no way to show that the ideas were his. Anyone could claim them now, could build upon his work and take the credit, and there would be no one to say otherwise.

I wanted to comfort him, to tell him that everything would be all right, that we would find a way to rebuild what had been lost. But I knew it was not true, and I think he knew it too. The world is not kind to men like Basil Crain, to dreamers who build in secret while other men take credit. And I am beginning to wonder whether the world is kind to women like me either, to women who dare to want more than their station allows, who dare to seek out connection and meaning in places where they are not supposed to look.

I am frightened, Grandmother. Frightened and confused and more alone than I have ever felt, more trapped than at any ball or dinner where I had to smile and pretend and be what everyone wanted me to be. But I will not stop writing to you, and I will not stop visiting Basil if I can help it, because the only thing that makes me feel real anymore is the knowledge that somewhere, someone is listening, someone who cares about my thoughts and my dreams rather than my dowry and my family name.

The worst part is that I cannot tell anyone what is happening. I cannot go to Mother and confess everything, because she would not understand, would not support me, would only weep and warn me about the damage I am doing to the family name. I cannot go to Father and beg him to call off the engagement, because he would not listen, would not care about my happiness, would only see dollar signs in the Whitmore connection. I cannot go to Constance and ask for help, because she is too afraid of Vivian, too afraid of social consequences, too afraid to take a stand for anything other than her own safety.

And I cannot go to Basil and tell him that we must find a way to be together, because he has already made it clear that he will not ask me to sacrifice my comfort for his poverty, that he will not be the one who drags me down from the gilded cage my father has built around me.

I do not know what will happen next. I do not know how this web will tighten or where it will lead. But I know that I cannot give up, not now, not when I have finally found something worth fighting for. Even if that fighting leads me to ruin, at least it will be my ruin, chosen by me rather than imposed by others.

I am thinking about the look on Basil's face when I told him about the break-in. It is not fair, Grandmother. It is not fair that a man with so much talent should be knocked down by men who have no imagination. It is not fair that the only thing standing between him and recognition is money, is connections.

And it is not fair that I love him, because loving him makes me complicit in my own destruction. Every moment I spend with him is a moment I spend building a future my family will never accept.

But I will not stop writing to you, Grandmother. Even if these letters never reach you, even if Father intercepts them and burns them before your eyes can read these words, I will keep writing, because the act of writing is the only thing that makes me feel like I still have a voice.

Your loving granddaughter,
Eleanor
