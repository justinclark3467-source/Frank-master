Dearest Grandmother,

I have not written in days. I have not known what to say, how to begin, or whether there is any point in putting pen to paper when the world seems determined to crush every spark of hope I have ever kindled. But I am writing tonight because something happened today that I cannot keep to myself, something so calculated and cruel that I have to believe it is the work of enemies I have not yet identified, people who want to see me destroyed not for what I have done but for who I am.

It started this morning. Father summoned me to the study, his face flushed with anger, his hands trembling as he held a newspaper. The Gazette. My engagement announcement, surrounded by the usual congratulatory notes from society friends. But that was not what had made him angry. No, what had made him angry was a small paragraph at the bottom of the page, a paragraph written in the gossip column by someone who signs only as "A Friend of the Family."

The paragraph suggested — in the carefully indirect language that gossipmongers use when they want to destroy without being caught — that I had been seen in "the company of an undesirable gentleman," that my "conduct" had been "the subject of comment," and that my parents would be "well advised to look into their daughter's associations." There was no name, no specific accusation, but the meaning was clear enough for anyone who knew anything about the fair or about Basil's workshop. Someone had been watching. Someone had been talking. And someone had decided to use my private life as a weapon.

Father demanded to know who this "undesirable gentleman" was. He demanded to know whether there was "any truth" in the "rumors," whether I had "done anything improper," whether I had "brought shame" on the family name. I denied everything, of course — I said I had no idea what he was talking about, that I had only ever attended the proper functions and visited the proper places. But Father looked at me with those cold, calculating eyes, and I could see that he did not believe me, that he had already decided what the truth was and was merely waiting for confirmation.

Mother tried tointervene, to quiet him, to suggest that the paragraph was probably the work of some jealous rival or some spiteful social enemy. But Father would not be quieted. He said he would "get to the bottom of this," that he would "find out what his daughter has been doing," that he would "not be made a fool of" by anyone, least of all by his own flesh and blood.

I know who did this. I know who must have done this, because there is no one else who had both the motive and the opportunity. Vivian Penworth. She has been watching me, noting my absences, cataloging my social deviations. And now she has struck, not directly — never directly, that is not her way — but through the quiet poison of public suggestion, the kind of gossip that sticks even when it cannot be proven, that stains even when it cannot be washed clean.

I went to Basil's workshop this afternoon, despite everything, despite the fear that someone might be watching, that my movements might be noted and reported. I had to see him. I had to warn him, though I did not know what warning I could possibly give. When I arrived, I found him hunched over his workbench, surrounded by what remained of his things, trying to rebuild from memory what had been stolen.

He told me something that made my blood run cold. He said a man had come to him two days ago, a well-dressed gentleman with a German accent, claiming to represent a consortium of industrialists who were "interested in his work." The man had asked questions, many questions — about the telephone, about the Lightning Engine, about the principles behind his inventions. Basil had answered, because he was proud of his work and wanted to share it with anyone who would listen. But now he wondered whether the questions had been more than curiosity, whether the interest had been more than commercial.

"They took everything," he said, "not because they want to build it themselves, but because they want to make sure no one can. They want to bury my work, Eleanor. They want to make sure it never sees the light of day."

I asked him what he intended to do, and he shook his head. There was nothing to do, he said. He had no money for lawyers, no connections to powerful friends, no proof of what had been stolen. The industrialists who had taken his work were too rich and too powerful to be challenged by a man like him, a man with nothing but ideas and determination.

I left his workshop with a heavy heart, knowing that the web was closing tighter, that the enemies were multiplying, that the social forces arrayed against me were growing stronger by the day. And I am beginning to wonder whether there is any way out, whether any path leads to freedom, or whether I am doomed to be trapped in this gilded cage forever.

I thought about confronting Vivian, about demanding to know why she has done this, why she has chosen to destroy me when I have never done anything to harm her. But I know how that conversation would go. She would deny everything with those wide, innocent eyes, would pretend to be wounded by the accusation, would turn the conversation around so that I would be the one apologizing for suggesting that she could be capable of such nastiness. She is too clever, too practiced in the art of social warfare, to ever be caught red-handed.

And even if I could prove it, what would I do with the proof? Bring it to Father? He would not believe me. Bring it to Mother? She would not support me. Bring it to society? They would not care. The Penworths are too well-established, too respected, too connected to ever be brought down by a young woman who has already been labeled as "difficult" and "unconventional" and "not quite right."

The worst part is that I cannot even be angry, not truly angry, because part of me understands why she has done it. I am everything she wishes she could be — independent, curious, willing to break the rules that she follows so meticulously. And she is everything that society demands of women — beautiful, well-dressed, perfectly behaved, perfectly empty. She hates me because she cannot be me, and she destroys me because she cannot admit her own envy.

But I will not stop writing to you, Grandmother. I will not stop hoping, even when hope seems foolish, even when the world tells me to despair. Because the only thing that keeps me sane is the knowledge that somewhere, someone is listening, someone who cares about my thoughts and my feelings and my dreams.

I do not know what tomorrow will bring. I do not know how much worse things will get before they get better. But I know that I will not give up, that I will keep fighting even when the fighting seems pointless.

Because love is worth fighting for. Hope is worth holding onto. And the truth, however buried, however suppressed, will someday come to light.

Your loving granddaughter,
Eleanor
