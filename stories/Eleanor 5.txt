Dearest Grandmother,

The announcement came today. Father told me at breakfast, as calmly as if he were discussing the weather, that the Whitmore family had accepted his proposal and that an engagement would be announced in next week's Gazette. The young Whitmore is "pleased," Father said, and his family is "satisfied," and this is "an excellent match" that I should be "grateful" for.

I said nothing. There was nothing to say. What words could possibly express what I feel, what defiance and despair and loneliness crashed through me like a wave breaking against stone? I merely nodded, as I have learned to do, and I excused myself from the table before my composure could crack, before the tears I was holding back could spill over and give Father another reason to worry about my "suitable" behavior.

Mother looked at me with something like pity in her eyes — the first time I have ever seen her look at me that way — but she said nothing. She never says anything. Not when Father decides my future, not when he arranges my life like a chess game, not when he trades me like a commodity to the highest bidder. Mother fades into the wallpaper, a quiet presence that sees everything and speaks to nothing, and I have never hated her more than I do tonight, for her silence is a kind of betrayal, a refusal to act when action is needed most.

I went to Basil's workshop. I had to see him. I had to tell someone, anyone, what is happening to me, and he is the only one who has ever really listened, the only one who has ever looked at me as if my thoughts and feelings mattered more than my dowry and my family name. The streets were crowded with people rushing home from work, their faces tired and careworn, and I sat in the hansom cab with my gloved hands clenched in my lap, rehearsing what I would say, how I would begin, how I would find the words to tell him what I have been too afraid to say aloud.

When I arrived, he was sitting at his workbench, staring at the empty space where his blueprints used to be. The room looked even more forlorn than before, as if the theft had taken something more than paper and ink — as if it had taken the very soul out of his work. He looked up when I entered, and his face was a mirror of my own despair, a reflection of the same hopelessness that was crushing my chest.

I told him about the engagement. I said it plainly, without drama, as if it were a fact of nature. And then I told him the rest — that I had been thinking of him, dreaming of him, that every moment with him was the only moment I felt truly alive. I told him I did not care about his money or his station, that I cared only about him, about his mind and his dreams and the way he looked at me as if I were a person rather than a prize.

He was quiet for a long moment. The silence was terrible, Grandmother, worse than any words could have been, because I knew what was coming before he said it. I could see it in his eyes, the resignation, the acceptance of his own inadequacy in the eyes of the world.

"Eleanor," he said, and his voice was gentle but also heavy, heavy with the weight of everything he could not say, "you know I care for you. You know I have never met anyone like you, anyone who makes me feel the way you make me feel. But I have nothing. No money, no connections, no prospects that would satisfy your father or anyone in his world. I cannot offer you a life worthy of your station. I cannot give you the things you deserve."

I told him I did not want those things. I told him I wanted only him, only his company, only the world he was building in his mind. But he shook his head, and he said, "You say that now. You mean it now. But you would grow to resent me, Eleanor. You would grow to hate the poverty, the uncertainty, the way the world would look at you and pity you for settling for less than you deserve. I cannot do that to you. I will not do that to you."

He turned away from me then, and he picked up a piece of wire from his workbench and began to twist it in his hands, and I knew that the conversation was over, that there was nothing more I could say to change his mind. I wanted to scream at him, to shake him, to tell him that he was wrong, that love was more important than money, that the world could go hang for all I cared. But I knew, too, that his reasons were not without foundation, that the world would judge us both, that my father would never accept him, that the social walls between us were too high to climb.

I left his workshop with tears streaming down my face, and I did not try to hide them. Let people stare. Let them gossip. Let them whisper their judgments. None of it matters anymore, because I have lost the only thing that made my life worth living, and I do not know how to go on pretending that everything is fine.

I walked home through the dark streets. I thought about the young Whitmore, the man I am to marry, the man I have spoken to twice and found utterly without substance. I thought about the life that awaits me, the endless round of dinner parties and social gatherings, the slow death of my spirit in a gilded cage.

And I thought about Basil, about the way his eyes lit up when he talked about the future, about the way his hands moved over his machines with such tenderness. I thought about what it would be like to wake up next to such a man, to build a life with him, however poor.

But it is not to be. He has made his choice, and I have made mine, and the two choices do not align. I am to be Mrs. Whitmore, with her comfortable home and her comfortable life and her slowly dying spirit, and he is to be alone with his machines and his dreams, and we will never know what might have been.

I do not know how to bear it, Grandmother. I do not know how to pretend that I am content with a future that holds no hope, no wonder, no love. But I also do not know how to fight against it, how to defy my father and my family and my entire world. I am trapped, more completely trapped than I have ever been, and I do not know which way to turn.

Your loving granddaughter,
Eleanor
