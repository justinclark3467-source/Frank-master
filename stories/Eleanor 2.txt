Dearest Grandmother,

I returned to the fair today, and I returned to his booth, just as I knew I would the moment I woke this morning. I told Mother I was visiting Constance Belmont, and for once she did not press for details. I think she is so exhausted by the endless round of society events and preparations for my coming engagement that she is grateful for any silence. She barely looked up from her knitting when I left.

The fair seemed different in the daylight, less dreamlike but more vivid, the White City revealed in all its white-painted glories. I walked past the Ferris wheel, which towers over everything yet somehow looks delicate, a spinning lace of iron and wood that seems to challenge the very concept of gravity. I thought of how strange it is that such a thing was built simply to show what could be done, not to serve any purpose but demonstration.

Basil was at his booth as before, surrounded by his wires and glass and the ever-present hum of his Lightning Engine. He recognized me immediately, which surprised me, for I am certain his booth sees hundreds of visitors each day. But he smiled and said, "You came back. I wasn't sure you would. I half thought I'd dreamed you."

Today he showed me other inventions. He has a small box that makes a mechanical bird sing, a canary rendered in brass and wire that hops from perch to perch and trills a tune he programmed into its tiny mechanism. He calls it a toy, but there is something in its tiny chest that suggests he sees a future where machines think and create.

"This is nothing," he said. "Wait until you see what comes next. Wait until you see the world I'm building in my mind."

I asked him what came next, and he grew animated. He told me about a world where messages do not travel by horse or rail but by electricity, where a man in Chicago could speak to a woman in New York as easily as speaking across a room.

"The telegraph was just the beginning," he said. "Soon we'll have phones in every home, pictures that move, machines that think. Everything you've ever imagined, everything you've ever dreamed — it can all be real."

I asked him where he learned to build such things, how a man without money or connections could possess such knowledge. His manner shifted. He became quieter. He told me his father was a clockmaker in a small town in Pennsylvania, and his mother was a schoolteacher who died when he was twelve. When his father died too, he left home at sixteen with nothing but his hands and his thinking.

"I've worked in telegraph offices and clock shops and factories," he said. "Everywhere and nowhere. Learning from every man who would teach me."

I wanted to ask him about money, about how he lives. But just then a gentleman approached the booth asking about the engine, and I lost my chance.

Walking away through the crowded Midway, past the streets lined with foreign villages and strange foods, I passed the Penworths. Vivian was wearing a gown of pale blue silk that must have cost a fortune, her hair arranged in the latest style. Arthur walked beside her in his formal attire. They did not see me, or if they did, they gave no sign of recognition. But I felt the weight of their presence nonetheless, the quiet judgment that always seems to emanate from people like them, people who notice everything and forget nothing.

I wonder, Grandmother, if this is how it will always be. If I am to be watched and noted and judged for every small deviation from the path they have laid for me. I wonder if wanting to know more about a man's thinking makes me improper, or if it is only improper when the man in question has no money and no name.

I am to be engaged soon, as you know. Father has mentioned Senator Whitmore's son, though he has not yet formally proposed. I have met the young man twice. He is polite and handsome and utterly, impossibly dull. He speaks of horses and weather and nothing of substance.

But I am thinking less of that today. I am thinking of the way his hands moved over his machine with such gentleness, as if he were coaxing life from dead metal. I am thinking of the way he looked at me when I asked a real question, as if I had surprised him by being more than a pretty face.

Perhaps I shall surprise him again tomorrow. Perhaps I shall surprise myself by finally finding the courage to be who I truly am rather than who they want me to be. I am thinking about what it would be like to be loved by such a man, to have someone look at me the way he looked at me when I told him I believed him, as if I had given him something precious.

Do write back if you can. I know you cannot, locked away as you are in your sickbed with Father controlling what goes in and out of the house. But I imagine your answers in my mind, and I think you would like him. I think you would find him as impossible and wonderful as I do, and I think you would tell me to be careful while also telling me to follow my heart.

I miss you, Grandmother. I wish you were here to see this world of wonders with me. Each day at the fair reveals something new, something that makes me ache for your gentle wisdom. I hope Father allows you to read these words. Until then, I will keep writing and thinking of you, dreaming of the day when I might share these moments with you in person, when I might introduce you to the strange and wonderful man who has captured my imagination and refuses to let go.

These thoughts consume me as I walk home through the evening air, the electric lights of the fair glowing in the distance like a promise of something more. I cannot help but wonder what tomorrow will bring, what new wonders Basil will show me, what new dreams he will inspire.

But I am also frightened, Grandmother. Frightened of the web that is slowly closing around me, frightened of the expectations that press upon me from every direction, frightened of a future that seems to hold nothing but gilded cages and arranged marriages and a life lived for the approval of others. Yet when I think of Basil, when I think of the way he looks at me as if I were a person rather than a prize, I feel a flicker of hope. And that hope, small as it is, is enough to keep me going.

Your loving granddaughter,
Eleanor
