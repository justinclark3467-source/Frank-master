Dearest Grandmother,

I am writing this late, by candlelight, with my hand cramped from holding the pen too tightly. The hour is well past midnight, and I should be asleep, but my mind will not quiet, racing with thoughts of the day that has just passed.

Today Basil took me to his workshop. He said it was not a place for ladies, that it was dirty and smelled of oil. I told him I was prepared to endure a little grime if it meant seeing the world he builds inside his mind, and he laughed.

The workshop is in a part of Chicago I have never seen, a district of brick buildings and smoke-stained walls where the air smells of oil and metal. His space is one room above a glassblower's shop, reached by a narrow staircase. The room itself is chaos — tables covered in tools, rolls of wire stacked against the walls, drawings pinned to every surface in overlapping layers. There is a cot in the corner with a blanket that has seen better days. A single window lets in the grey light of the city, dim and filtered through the soot.

He apologized for the mess, for the appearance of his sanctuary, as though he were suddenly aware of how this must look to someone of my background. But I did not see a mess. I saw a life — the physical proof of everything he has told me, every drawing and model and attempt at something new that the world has not yet learned to want.

On his workbench, I saw the skeleton of what he calls his telephone, a device to carry the human voice across distances. It looked like nothing — a box with a mouth and an ear — but he explained how it works, the principles of sound and current that make it possible. When he spoke into it and I listened on the other end, his voice came through as clearly as if he were standing right beside me. I laughed, Grandmother. I could not help it. The sound of his voice, tinny and strange but undeniably his, coming from that plain box — it was like magic.

"This is only the beginning," he said. "Wait until you see what we will have in ten years. In twenty. The world will not recognize itself. We will talk across oceans. We will see each other's faces through wires. We will light cities without flames and move faster than horses and fly, Eleanor — we will actually fly."

I asked him what he wanted most in the world, and I expected him to say money or fame or recognition. Instead, he was quiet for a long moment, and then he said, "I want to be believed. I want someone to look at what I have made and say, 'Yes, this matters. Yes, you are right to spend your life on this.' I am tired of being laughed at, Eleanor. I am tired of building things in secret while other men take credit for what they do not understand."

I told him I believed him. I said it plainly, without artifice, as if it were the simplest truth, and I met his eyes as I said it. And his face — oh, his face. The vulnerability there was almost too much to bear, the naked emotion in his eyes overwhelming in its intensity. He looked at me as if I had given him something precious, something he had been waiting years to receive.

"Eleanor," he said — my first name spoken aloud, which no man outside my family has ever done — "you are unlike anyone I have ever met. You ask real questions, and you listen to the answers, and you don't look at me like I'm a fool or a madman. I don't know what you are. But I'm grateful you exist. I don't know what I'd do without you."

I should have been shocked. I should have corrected him, reminded him of the distance between us. But I was not shocked. I was, for the first time in my life, exactly where I wanted to be, saying exactly what I wanted to say, with exactly the person I wanted to say it to.

We talked for another hour, perhaps more. He showed me his plans, his dreams, the things he has sketched but not yet built. And when it was time for me to go, because the light was fading and I knew Mother would soon send someone to search for me, he walked me to the door of his workshop and said, "Will you come again? I don't have much to offer you but this — this room, these ideas, this world I'm building in my mind. But it's yours if you want it. Everything I have is yours if you want it."

I said yes.

When I arrived home, Mother met me at the door with a strange expression — not anger, not suspicion, but something more like confusion. She said the Penworths were asking after me at the tea party this afternoon — Vivian remarked that I had been absent from the last two social functions and wondered if I was well.

I told her I was well, that I had been visiting Constance as I claimed. But Mother looked at me as if she knew I was lying, and I confess I was — lying, that is, about where I had truly been and who I had truly been with.

I do not know what is happening to me, Grandmother. I only know that when I am with Basil Crain, I feel more alive than I have ever felt at any dinner-party or ball or gathering, more seen and heard and valued for my mind rather than my dowry. And I am terrified of what will happen if anyone finds out — if Father learns where I have been, if Mother discovers the truth, if the Penworths spread their poison through the social circles that control my fate.

But I am also, for the first time, unafraid. I am terrified and unafraid at the same time, and I do not know what to do with that contradiction except hold it close and hope it does not destroy me. Perhaps this is what it feels like to truly live, to stake everything on a connection that society says is impossible, to choose the person you want to be rather than the person others have decided you should be.

I miss you, Grandmother. I wish you were here to guide me through this strange new world I am stumbling into. Each day brings new wonders and new fears. I hold your memory close and hope you can feel my love across the distance that separates us, hope that somehow these words reach you and bring you comfort as they bring me relief. I wonder if you ever felt this way, if you ever knew this kind of all-consuming passion that makes everything else seem pale and meaningless. I wonder if you will tell me, when I finally see you again, whether I am being brave or foolish. Perhaps they are the same thing.

Your loving granddaughter,
Eleanor
